The analytical problem this report solves

In the first days of the Iran-Israel conflict, the Strait of Hormuz closed and Qatar shut down Ras Laffan, the world's largest LNG export complex. That single event removed approximately 20 per cent of global LNG supply from the market. The final tankers that loaded before the blockade are now arriving at their destinations.

The real shock has not yet been felt. And the dominant policy response — redirect supply from the US, Australia, and other producers to fill the gap — rests on an assumption that does not survive arithmetic. This report makes the case that the gap cannot be filled. It will be redistributed as pain. The question is who absorbs it, in what sequence, and what the durable consequences are for the organisations and markets involved.

Key findings

Finding 01

The substitution arithmetic does not work. No combination of US, Australian, or other available supply comes close to replacing Qatar's volumes. The numbers rule it out before the policy debate begins.

Finding 02

The compatibility problem compounds the volume problem. Qatar's LNG has specific compositional characteristics that require terminal modifications before alternative supply can be received at scale. This turns a volume shock into a multi-year infrastructure problem.

Finding 03

Pain will fall in sequence — Pakistan first, then South and Southeast Asia, then Europe and Northeast Asia competing for the same residual spot cargoes. Understanding that sequence is the most operationally useful analysis available right now.

Finding 04

The second-order effects are underpriced. Qatar supplies 30 per cent of global helium. Korea sources 65 per cent of its helium from Qatar. Helium is a semiconductor input. This is not an energy story — it is a supply chain story that energy analysts have missed.

Finding 05

Demand destruction is the base case, not a tail risk. Given the supply arithmetic, it is the mechanism through which the market will clear. The policy question is not whether it occurs but how it is distributed and managed.

Finding 06

The crisis will permanently restructure Asian LNG contracting. Even if Ras Laffan restarts, the assumption that it was inviolable is gone. Force majeure declarations on 20-year contracts will drive replacement contracting that reshapes the supply architecture for a decade.

⚠ Non-obvious risk

The crisis has exposed a vulnerability that sits entirely outside energy sector analysis: Qatar supplies approximately 30 per cent of global helium, and South Korea sources around 65 per cent of its helium from Qatar. Helium is a critical input in semiconductor manufacturing. If supply restrictions extend beyond six weeks — the threshold that current inventories can sustain — Korean chipmakers face production schedule adjustments. That consequence has not been priced into most risk assessments.
