⚙ The core analytical conclusion

The duties remain in place. Conditional relief and investment requirements add further obligations. The Volkswagen undertaking shows how exemption from a border charge can depend on continuing commitments inside the Union. Existing investment screening already matters, while the Industrial Accelerator Act would add a separate set of conditions if enacted. This report examines how those requirements can accumulate, what China’s earlier investment regime achieved, and how Chinese technology export controls could affect firms’ ability to meet the proposed European conditions.

The analytical problem this report solves

In February 2026, the European Commission accepted a model-specific price undertaking jointly offered by Volkswagen (Anhui) and SEAT for the China-built CUPRA Tavascan. Compliant covered shipments are exempt from the countervailing duty. The arrangement includes a minimum import price, an annual quota, a specified import channel, sales reporting and commitments concerning ongoing and new BEV investment projects in the EU. Ordinary customs duties and other applicable charges remain.

Paying the duty, negotiating an undertaking and developing European production create different obligations. An undertaking requires continuing compliance. Local production requires a separate assessment of the goods imported, their customs classification and origin, alongside applicable investment approvals. The proposed Industrial Accelerator Act would add further conditions for qualifying investments. The report separates these requirements so firms can assess the costs, responsibilities and uncertainties attached to each route.

What changed in the October update

A wider industrial footprint, with a clearer distinction between presence and production. The accompanying map of Chinese BEV and battery activity in Europe now contains 75 project and presence records across 18 territories, within a 31-territory frame. The review adds 13 records to the previous file baseline. Of 42 ongoing, in-scope manufacturing records, 17 have documented production. Shared factory phases, proposals, historical projects and R&D presence are recorded separately, so 75 is not a physical-factory count.

The additions include Gotion’s proposed participation in Sagunto and its upstream projects in Valladolid, documented component production at Evoring in Hungary, and Volvo’s plant under construction near Košice. Existing records also distinguish CATL’s September cell trials at Debrecen from serial production, and BYD’s trial-stage activity at Szeged from commercial output.

A more precise account of the regulatory position. The update separates the Commission’s March Industrial Accelerator Act proposal from later Council negotiating texts that were not directly accessible at the time of review. It covers the adopted revision of the EU investment-screening framework and its phased application, the technical scope and timing of China’s battery-related controls, and the WTO challenge to the BEV duties.

It also addresses reported pressure for measures on plug-in hybrids. As of the report’s October 4 evidence review, no formal PHEV investigation notice had been documented. The report distinguishes press reporting and political proposals from measures in force.

Key findings

Finding 01

The Volkswagen undertaking makes duty relief conditional on continuing compliance. Its published terms cover price, volume, the import channel, reporting and EU investment projects. Price undertakings are established trade defense practice. The explicit investment linkage adds a further dimension, but one case does not establish the terms of future undertakings.

Finding 02

The Industrial Accelerator Act remains a proposal. The Commission’s March text would apply to qualifying manufacturing investments above EUR 100 million where the investor’s country accounts for more than 40 percent of relevant global capacity. Approval would require the workforce condition plus at least three other criteria. Sector, control, exclusions and timing also matter.

Finding 03

National authorities would issue investment decisions, with Commission involvement in specified cases. The March proposal includes assessment above EUR 1 billion, strategic and qualifying requested-assessment routes below that figure, and powers in multi-state disputes. Announced project cost alone does not establish notification liability or retrospective application.

Finding 04

China’s automotive investment regime provides a precedent, but the evidence on its results is mixed. Studies measure different outcomes over different periods. Joint ventures and affiliated producers gained capabilities in several accounts, while worker mobility and supplier networks also mattered. A licensing condition alone cannot be assumed to deliver the same gains across an industry.

Finding 05

The Commission now proposes investment conditions similar to those it criticized China for imposing on European firms in 2019. Its own impact assessment identifies the Chinese precedent. For firms, the practical lesson is to plan against the operative provisions and their legal status, while treating the political rationale as context.

Finding 06

Chinese technology export controls could affect compliance with a European licensing condition. The July 2025 catalogue covers specified cathode, precursor and lithium-processing technologies. A separate battery and graphite package is suspended until November 10, 2026. The European licensing criterion is optional in the March proposal, and exposure depends on the actual technology transferred.
