The core analytical question

Can a pharmaceutical firm meet tomorrow’s conditions for access to the US market without losing access to the Chinese capabilities needed to deliver the medicine? A license secures rights. The harder question is whether the firm has the knowledge, capacity and regulatory freedom of action to exercise them if Washington or Beijing changes the rules.

The investment problem behind the licensing boom

Pharmaceutical firms are committing substantial capital to Chinese innovation while Washington revisits the terms on which Chinese technology can participate in the American economy. Beijing, meanwhile, has shown that it intends to decide which capabilities can leave its jurisdiction.

The exposure extends beyond whether a deal is permissible today. Development, manufacturing and supply may continue for years after signing. This report examines how changes in trade and investment policy could reach those continuing relationships, drawing on US precedents in other sectors and China’s controls over technology outflows.

Key findings

Finding 01

A deal can close without settling its exposure. The rights acquired at signing may still depend on development work, manufacturing knowhow or supply that a Chinese partner must continue to provide. The investment question is what happens if the conditions governing that contribution change.

Finding 02

An American factory may answer only part of the problem. Precedents from solar manufacturing, connected vehicles and communications equipment show why location alone may not satisfy Washington. Control, production origin and continuing technology relationships can also determine access.

Finding 03

Negotiated relief has limits. Investment and pricing commitments can help secure relief under one policy instrument. They do not necessarily resolve separate procurement, investment or technology-transfer restrictions, or bind a successor administration.

Finding 04

Beijing has a say in the adjustment. Moving production may require knowledge, data and practical support that cannot simply be moved with the equipment. China’s controls matter to the feasibility of a relocation strategy as well as its cost.

Finding 05

Three transactions reveal different dependencies. AstraZeneca–Summit, BMS–Hengrui and AstraZeneca–CSPC locate the exposure in continuing supply, joint development and discovery capabilities. Their disclosed commitments establish commercial stakes, not a common loss estimate.

Finding 06

The next policy step matters. The report distinguishes enacted measures, operative restrictions, legislative proposals and reported deliberations. Its scenarios identify what would have to change before a disclosed dependency became a commercial constraint.
