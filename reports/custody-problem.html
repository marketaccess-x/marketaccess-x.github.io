⚙ The core analytical conclusion

Vendor diversification answers a competition problem; capability custody answers a jurisdiction problem. What changed on June 12, 2026 is that the jurisdiction problem became the live one. Keeping three US frontier labs in the mix instead of one buys no protection when a single directive can reach all of them through the jurisdiction they share. The only capability a state cannot pull from the market is an open weight already in the wild. This report shows how to tell which of your AI dependencies can be withdrawn, and what to do about the ones that can.

The analytical problem this report solves

On June 12, 2026, the US Commerce Department ordered Anthropic to stop supplying its two most capable AI models to any non-US person, and within hours the company disabled them for every customer on Earth. It was the first time the United States reached past chips and past companies to pull a deployed frontier model off the global market by directive. For a firm that had built a product on that model, the capability did not degrade or get more expensive. It vanished overnight.

Most firms respond to this kind of shock by diversifying vendors, and against this risk that does almost nothing. The report separates two risks firms routinely conflate: yank-risk, the danger that a state cuts off access to a capability, and compliance-risk, the danger that a state makes a firm's use of a capability unlawful. They pull in opposite directions, and the conventional hedge addresses neither. The answer is capability custody: for the workloads where a sudden cutoff would be existential, depending on a capability that no single state can withdraw from the market.

Key findings

Finding 01

The risk that materialized is yank-risk, not competition risk. A state can withdraw access to an AI capability with little notice, and on June 12 the US did exactly that. Diversifying across vendors does not address it, because every US frontier lab sits behind the same jurisdiction and the same discretionary authority.

Finding 02

Enforceability does not simply fall as the chokepoint climbs the stack; it splits. Closed, API-served models stay controllable through the provider the state can compel directly. Open weights, once published, cannot be recalled by any state. The open–closed line within the model layer is where control is won or lost.

Finding 03

The yank-risk is administration-independent. The prior administration tried to wall model weights in by rule, and the rule was rescinded; the current administration sells advanced chips to Chinese firms and deregulates AI at home, and it still pulled a frontier model from allied users overnight. No political configuration removes the exposure.

Finding 04

The courts are a real check, but slow, uneven, and not the buyer's to invoke. An American provider can litigate a withdrawal and may win, but correction takes months, national-security framing is where courts defer most, and the foreign firm depending on the model has no standing in the case.

Finding 05

The same move has a precedent. After 1999, US security controls on commercial satellites pushed foreign buyers to engineer out US content and treat jurisdiction-independence as a purchasing criterion. The mechanism is uncontested, and it is replaying: independence from US jurisdiction is the feature an open-weight model now offers.

Finding 06

The answer is capability custody along a continuum, not buying GPUs. For most firms it means open weights served by a third party: yank-immune, re-sourceable, zero capital. Self-hosting is a costly far end reserved for genuine sovereignty or residency drivers. Custody is a capability-verified hedge, not a free swap.
