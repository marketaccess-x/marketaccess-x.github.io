⚙ The core analytical conclusion

The cost that can break a firm is not the new tariff. It is the collision between US forced-labor enforcement and a Chinese countermeasure stack built to punish the act of compliance itself. The conduct US enforcement demands under UFLPA and Section 307, tracing and terminating tainted suppliers, is the precise conduct Decree 834, Decree 835, the AFSL, and the Unreliable Entity List are built to deter. That collision does not appear on any duty schedule. This report maps where it bites, and where it does not.

The analytical problem this report solves

On June 2, 2026, USTR found all 60 investigated economies, accounting for 99.4% of US imports, actionable for failing to prohibit or effectively enforce against forced labor. The finding is universal in effect, and no sourcing geography offers an exit. The proposed tariff is tiered by compliance posture, but compliance buys a 2.5-percentage-point discount, not an exit.

The cost that can break a firm does not arrive as a tariff at all. It arrives where the conduct US enforcement demands, tracing and terminating tainted suppliers, is the precise conduct Chinese law is now built to punish. This report identifies who that collision binds, and sorts real inputs into three zones: genuinely exitable, exitable only slowly and at high cost, and structurally trapped. The trap binds a specific firm, the multinational with simultaneous US-market exposure and Chinese operations or supply chain. It is the intersection of the two exposures, not the union.

Key findings

Finding 01

The June 2 finding is universal in effect and durable on its statutory text. USTR found all 60 economies actionable under the persistent-pattern-of-forced-labor clause. The Section 301 hook is more litigation-durable than the struck-down IEEPA and Section 122 authorities because it names duties as a remedy and forced labor as a covered practice.

Finding 02

Compliance buys a discount, not an exit. An Agreement on Reciprocal Trade commitment moves an economy from the 12.5% band to 10%, but all nine ART signatories were found actionable anyway. A signed commitment earns the same 10% as an enacted ban, which rewards a sign-and-slow-walk response.

Finding 03

The cost that can break a firm is not a tariff. A tariff can be priced, passed through, or engineered around. The conflict-of-laws trap offers no number at all, because it is not a cost but a prohibition, and the firm's own compliance conduct is what springs it.

Finding 04

The two jaws are real and opposed. The conduct UFLPA and Section 307 demand, supply-chain tracing, supplier audits, supplier termination, is the conduct Decree 834, Decree 835, the AFSL and Order 803, and the Unreliable Entity List are built to deter and punish.

Finding 05

The trap is architecture in place, not enforcement under way. As of June 2026, no action under the countermeasure stack has targeted due diligence, and the Decree 835 Malicious Entity List is empty. But adjacent conduct has already been punished through older instruments: the 2023 Mintz raid and fine, the Capvision crackdown, the expanded Counter-Espionage Law.

Finding 06

Polysilicon is the anchor: trapped on volume and cost, with a claimed exit that has not yet earned the name. China produced roughly 95% of solar-grade polysilicon in 2024, and non-China material carries an almost fourfold price premium. United Solar in Oman is a dated, financed escape hatch, not proof the clock has run out.
