The Price of Admission: The US FCC's Optical Transceiver Rule Is Not About China

5 August 2026 Technology Controls Supply Chain US-China
The reported US restriction on Chinese optical transceivers is mostly being read as a test of how Chinese a module is. The instrument the FCC has spent eight months building tests something else: whether the module is American. A plant in Thailand fails that on the same terms as a plant in Suzhou.

On August 4, Reuters reported that the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) is in the process of drafting restrictions on imports of new Chinese optical transceiver models for data centers, with a rule possibly coming into effect later this year. The analysis that followed converged on how the rule will define Chinese: by corporate parent ownership, or by geographic point of origin. That distinction matters because Chinese module makers have moved final assembly, optical alignment and testing for their US allocations into Thailand precisely to survive measures targeting origin. Both definitions assume Washington is drawing a line around China, but in reality the line that is being drawn is around the United States.

Four product categories have been added to the FCC's Covered List in the past eight months: unmanned aircraft systems in December, consumer routers in March, and connected power inverters together with advanced robotic devices in July. Each prohibits an entire category of products from receiving FCC equipment authorization, which in practice means these cannot be imported or sold in the United States. Transceivers would be the fifth.

The operative term in each listing is “foreign produced,” and it does not mean what a trade lawyer would assume. Foreign produced in FCC parlance means anything failing to qualify as a domestic end product under the Buy American standard at 48 CFR 25.101(a): manufactured in the United States, with domestic components exceeding 65 percent of total component cost. The FCC has said plainly that the nationality of the producer is not relevant and that the action is country neutral. The router determination adds that production covers any major stage of the process, including manufacturing, assembly, design and development.

So the line is not drawn between Chinese and non-Chinese firms, nor between Chinese-owned and foreign-owned ones. It is drawn between the United States and everywhere else, which catches a module assembled in Penang by a Japanese company as surely as one assembled in Suzhou. Southeast Asian capacity works against tariffs, which are applied at the border on the basis of country of origin. It does nothing against a prohibition that treats all foreign origin alike. The design and development language closes what little space remains, since relocating final assembly leaves the earlier stages where they were.

Which raises the question of how anything gets imported at all.

Relief runs through a Conditional Approval, adjudicated by the Department of War, the Department of Homeland Security, or both depending on the category. Applicants submit what the FCC describes as a detailed, time-bound plan to establish or expand manufacturing in the United States. By late July roughly eighteen router and more than twenty drone approvals had been issued, to firms including Netgear, Nokia and Amazon's eero. In July the FCC stripped the termination dates from drone approvals, making them indefinite for as long as the holder honors its onshoring commitment.

That is the structure. A prohibition covering all foreign production without regard to nationality, and an exemption available in exchange for an enforceable commitment to build in the United States, with access lasting only as long as the commitment is honored. The application annex contains no security test at all: no code audit, no firmware attestation, no mechanism for demonstrating that a device cannot be manipulated remotely. An applicant establishes that it poses no unacceptable risk by disclosing its supply chain and committing to move production. It is neither a tariff nor an export control. It sets a price for entry to the American market, and the price is denominated in domestic factories.

None of which makes the transceiver rule inevitable or settles its shape. The drone listing has been amended repeatedly since December, most recently to carve out toy drones after a lobbying campaign the Toy Association publicly claimed credit for. No published approval notice so far names a Chinese-headquartered parent among the recipients, but the criteria have never been published, so that is an absence in the record rather than a stated rule.

Chinese module makers build on American silicon. The digital signal processors come from Broadcom and Marvell, the lasers from Lumentum and Coherent. So the firms best placed to gain share from a ban are currently selling parts to the firms it would exclude, which is a constraint on how far Washington will want to push this.

There is precedent for retreat. Reuters reported in February that the Commerce Department had shelved a group of proposed China import restrictions, one of them targeting data center equipment, following the understanding reached at Busan in October 2025. What lapsed at Commerce did not lapse at the FCC, which added drones in December, routers in March, and inverters and robots in July. Beijing's suspension of its ban on gallium, germanium and antimony exports to the United States runs only until November 27, and the FCC wants a transceiver rule this year. Those two clocks will meet. Whatever comes of that, the machinery is the more durable development: used four times in eight months, expanding into product classes that have nothing to do with communications as the term is ordinarily understood, and never once turning on where a company is from. Firms that spent the tariff war building factories in third countries hedged against the instrument they were shown. The one they are getting only cares whether the factory is American.
