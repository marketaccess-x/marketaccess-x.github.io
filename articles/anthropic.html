Anthropic Falls Victim to the Weaponization of Supply Chain Risk

28 February 2026 AI Policy Supply Chain Risk Trade Law Updated 31 March 2026
Anthropic's unprecedented designation as a supply chain risk was legally dubious from the start. A federal judge has now said so in writing — but the battle is far from over.

On February 28, 2026, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth declared Anthropic a "supply chain risk to national security" — marking the first time in American history this designation had been applied to a domestic company. The move came after Anthropic refused to remove two guardrails from its Claude model: prohibitions on mass domestic surveillance of Americans, and deployment in fully autonomous weapons systems. President Trump simultaneously directed all federal agencies to cease using Anthropic's technology.

The immediate trigger was a commercial contract dispute dressed up in national security language. But the choice of instrument matters enormously. The supply chain risk designation under 10 USC 3252 is a tool historically reserved for foreign adversaries — aimed at hardware and software embedded in critical infrastructure by entities whose loyalties lie elsewhere. Applying it to a domestic AI company for declining contract terms was a qualitative departure from prior practice, with downstream effects extending well beyond Anthropic's balance sheet.

The commercial reach was deliberately ambiguous. Any company doing business with the Pentagon suddenly faced a binary choice: demonstrate it has no commercial relationship with Anthropic, or risk its government contracts. Claude became radioactive not because it posed a security risk, but because association with it created legal and reputational exposure. That chilling effect was the real instrument of leverage — and it operated regardless of whether the designation ever survived legal challenge.

Legal soundness is beside the point in the short run. General counsels across corporate America will make the conservative choices that minimize risk exposure — and that is precisely the intent.

The deeper problem is what this episode signals for any company that negotiates with the US government over the terms on which its technology can be used. A designation historically reserved for adversaries, applied retributively to a domestic company for asserting its contractual position, degrades the credibility of the instrument at the precise moment the United States most needs it — including in genuine disputes with suppliers who actually do have ties to foreign governments.

31 Mar 2026

On March 26, U.S. District Judge Rita F. Lin granted Anthropic a preliminary injunction, blocking the designation and ruling it constituted First Amendment retaliation. Internal DoW records showed Anthropic was designated for its "hostile manner through the press" — not any security risk. The ruling vindicates the analysis above, but a parallel FASCSA designation remains in force, the government has signaled a Ninth Circuit appeal, and full resolution is likely more than a year away.

The irony remains sharp. In the week the administration declared the AI race with China equivalent to the space race, it moved to neutralize one of its own most capable competitors. The courts have begun to push back. But the framework that produced this designation is intact, and the precedent question — whether supply chain risk authorities can be turned on domestic companies that decline to comply on the government's terms — is far from settled.
