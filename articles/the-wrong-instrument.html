The Wrong Instrument: Europe's Chemicals Crisis Was Made in Brussels Too

9 June 2026 Trade Defense Industrial Policy EU-China
As the Commission contemplates new trade restrictions on Chinese chemical imports, it is worth remembering that it does so partly because the alternative would carry a far higher political cost at home. The choices that would actually help the European chemical industry were named two years ago, and studiously avoided ever since.

Ahead of the June 18-19 European Council meeting, Brussels is preparing to defend its chemicals industry with tariffs and quotas. And again we're hearing a familiar story: cheap Chinese imports are killing European producers, and trade barriers are the answer. This narrative conveniently pins the blame on someone else and diverts attention from the part of the problem that was wholly home-grown.

Start with what Europe charges its own producers before a single import arrives. European factories pay two to three times more for electricity than competitors in the United States or China. Energy-intensive industries also carry one of the world's highest carbon prices, which their foreign rivals do not. These were deliberate choices, made to buy a cleaner energy system, and there are honest reasons to defend them. But they are still a weight strapped to the back of every European producer. A tariff on imports does nothing to alleviate this. Rather, it raises the price of the imported product, which lets domestic producers raise theirs in turn and does nothing to lower the cost of making PVC in Belgium, or anywhere else in the bloc.

So why reach for the tariff anyway? Because it is the one move that asks nothing hard of anyone in Brussels.

The hard moves were named nearly two years ago, in Mario Draghi's competitiveness report, which set out in detail what ails European industry: power prices chained to the cost of gas, an energy market too fragmented to deliver cheap electricity, and no serious money behind the industries European policymakers claim they want to save. Fixing any of that means trade-offs that involve real political pain. Cheaper, integrated electricity requires member states to give up control over their own power grids, which they refuse to do. The richer northern members now subsidize their own industries while poorer members cannot afford to, quietly pulling the single market apart. Every one of these fixes creates winners and losers with names and votes.

A tariff imposes no such cost. It is paid by importers, attributed to Beijing, and requires no government to surrender anything to Brussels or to its neighbors. That, put simply, is the appeal of trade barriers: the path of least resistance dressed up as resolve.

Draghi, it is worth remembering, did not rule out trade defense. He accepted it, but only as careful, last-resort protection, and only alongside the harder work of cutting energy costs and funding the transition. He also warned against propping up industries simply because they are loud, and noted that in some sectors foreign producers are now so far ahead that trying to claw back production at home is money better spent elsewhere. Commodity PVC, made more cheaply almost anywhere with cheaper power, sits uncomfortably close to that category. The tariff lets Europe avoid the awkward question of which industries it can realistically still win.

None of this means China is blameless; there are, after all, no innocent parties in trade policy, except perhaps the consumer. There is a genuine glut of Chinese chemical capacity, built for a construction boom that has since collapsed and is now looking for export markets. Trade defense against genuinely unfair pricing (where such pricing can actually be demonstrated) is a legitimate tool, and Europe, like any other WTO member, is entitled to use it. The point is narrower: a tariff treats the symptom while leaving the disease untouched. It buys a European PVC producer a little time without making it any cheaper to run a plant in Europe.

And the bill does not land on China. It lands on the European manufacturers downstream who now pay more for their raw materials, and on the workers in countries that cannot match the subsidies of their wealthier neighbors. Europe was handed a diagnosis and a list of difficult cures. It is reaching instead for the one remedy that requires no courage, and calling it a defense against China.
